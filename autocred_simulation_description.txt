Simulation description: supermarket pedestrian service and checkout scenario

This simulation represents pedestrian movement inside and around a supermarket-like
environment using a Social Force Model style crowd dynamics implementation.
The scenario is designed to test whether pedestrian agents can complete a
multi-stage shopping route while maintaining physically and socially plausible
motion in a constrained indoor layout.

Overall scenario objective

Pedestrians must enter the supermarket, travel to a preassigned service or item
collection point, wait briefly to represent item pickup/service, proceed to a
checkout area, and then leave the store through an exit. A credible simulation
should show pedestrians completing this route efficiently while preserving
realistic local interactions, smooth goal-directed motion, and reasonable
clearance from other pedestrians, racks, walls, and gate boundaries.

Environment and layout

The environment is a two-dimensional supermarket floor plan. The floor contains
walkable areas, static obstacles, rack-like blocked regions, entry/exit gates,
orange gate/transition regions, checkout/service locations, and boundary walls.
Pedestrians are not expected to move through blocked obstacle regions or outside
the valid store layout. The route planner provides waypoint sequences through
walkable space, and the Social Force Model controls continuous pedestrian
motion between those waypoints.

Pedestrian agents

Each pedestrian is modeled as a circular body with a physical radius, position,
velocity, desired speed, relaxation time, current target waypoint, and final
route objective. Pedestrians may start inside the store at different shopping
stages or outside the store waiting to enter. Outside pedestrians enter according
to staggered entry times. Once active, each pedestrian should move toward the
current route waypoint, update to the next waypoint when the current target is
reached or passed, and eventually leave through a valid exit.

Route and task sequence

Each pedestrian has a preassigned service point. The intended task sequence is:

1. Enter the supermarket through a valid entry gate if the pedestrian starts
   outside.
2. Move through the walkable route network toward the assigned service or item
   collection point.
3. Pause briefly at the service point to represent collecting the item.
4. Continue toward the checkout portion of the route.
5. Move from checkout toward the exit route.
6. Leave the store through a valid exit after completing the required route
   sequence.

The scenario should not be judged only by whether pedestrians eventually leave.
The credibility of the path taken matters. A pedestrian that reaches the exit
by cutting through racks, bypassing checkout, teleporting, oscillating
unnaturally, or using physically implausible movement should be considered less
credible even if aggregate completion metrics look good.

Social Force Model expectations

Pedestrian motion should be explainable by the modeled Social Force components:
driving acceleration toward the current goal or waypoint, social repulsion from
nearby pedestrians, and obstacle/wall repulsion from static geometry. The
observed acceleration should generally agree with the acceleration predicted by
these modeled components, except during explicitly defined discrete events such
as spawning, route stage changes, waiting at service, boundary correction, or
leaving the environment.

Expected credible behaviour

A credible run should exhibit the following behaviours:

- Pedestrians generally move toward their current waypoint and make steady
  progress through the intended entry-service-checkout-exit sequence.
- Trajectories are smooth at the simulation time scale, without sudden jumps,
  unrealistic velocity reversals, high-frequency jitter, or repeated stop-start
  oscillation that is not caused by congestion or service waiting.
- Speeds remain in a plausible pedestrian range and are consistent with the
  sampled desired-speed distribution and maximum-speed limits.
- Accelerations remain physically plausible and are mostly explained by the
  Social Force acceleration components recorded in the time-series data.
- Pedestrians maintain personal space when possible and begin avoidance before
  severe body overlap occurs.
- Short interactions in crowded or narrow regions may occur, but sustained
  overlap, deep overlap, and long periods of body interpenetration indicate
  poor credibility.
- Agents avoid static obstacles, rack regions, walls, and invalid areas rather
  than passing through them.
- Congestion near gates, service points, or checkout may slow movement, but it
  should not produce nonphysical escape behaviour, indefinite trapping, or
  large unexplained acceleration spikes.
- Pedestrians that are waiting at service points should remain locally stable
  instead of drifting, vibrating, or pushing through other agents.
- Exiting pedestrians should disappear or become inactive only after completing
  the intended route sequence and reaching a valid exit region.

Expected problematic behaviour

The following patterns should reduce plausibility:

- Pedestrians reach the final goal while skipping the assigned service point or
  checkout sequence.
- Pedestrians move away from their current goal for long periods without a
  clear interaction or recovery reason.
- Agents pass through racks, walls, obstacles, or non-walkable layout regions.
- Overlap metrics improve only because pedestrians are pushed unnaturally far
  away from one another, freeze, or avoid entering the store.
- Pedestrians exhibit extreme acceleration, abrupt direction changes, visible
  jitter, or velocity clipping artifacts not explained by the SFM terms.
- The SFM residual is large for many active agents during ordinary movement,
  meaning observed acceleration is not explained by the recorded driving,
  social, and obstacle components.
- Nearest-neighbor distances collapse repeatedly in crowded regions without
  anticipatory slowing or steering.
- Time-to-collision is frequently small while social repulsion remains weak or
  while agents continue accelerating into one another.
- Boundary correction or route recovery dominates the motion rather than acting
  as an occasional safeguard.
- High goal reach rate is achieved by behaviour that violates the conceptual
  shopping route or the physical layout.

Credibility assessment focus

You should judge this scenario using the time-series data and generated
plots with emphasis on both task success and mechanism plausibility. The
important question is not only whether numerical target metrics improve, but
whether the movement remains credible for a Social Force Model of pedestrians
performing a supermarket entry, service, checkout, and exit task.

The most important evidence should include:

- Progress through the route over time.
- Position and trajectory patterns in relation to goals and obstacles.
- Speed and acceleration distributions over time.
- Agreement between observed acceleration and SFM-predicted acceleration.
- Personal-space, nearest-neighbor, overlap-depth, and time-to-collision
  patterns.
- Differences between normal movement and explicit event states such as
  inactive, spawned, active, waiting, route_recovery, boundary_clipped, and
  reached_goal.

A candidate model should be considered plausible only if it improves or
preserves task performance while remaining consistent with the intended Social
Force Model mechanisms, the supermarket task sequence, and the physical
constraints of the environment.
